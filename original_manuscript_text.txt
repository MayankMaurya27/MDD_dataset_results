Automated Detection of Major Depressive Disorder Using SPWVD-Based EEG Time-Frequency Images and Deep Learning Features
Abstract
Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a significant global health concern, especially after the COVID-19 epidemic. Electroencephalography (EEG) has emerged as a valuable tool for diagnosing MDD, manual screening of EEG data is a time-consuming and subject to variability. In this paper, we propose an innovative integrated machine-learning system that harnesses EEG sensor data to automate the detection of MDD. By leveraging advanced machine learning algorithms, our system aims to revolutionize the diagnostic process and improve patient outcomes. This work introduce a deep learning-based feature extraction and classification technique that utilizes machine learning tactics to automate the screening of depressive EEG sensor data. By applying the Smoothed Pseudo Wigner-Ville distribution, we extract time-frequency (T-F) images from the input data, which are then fed into well-known deep learning models to obtain deep features. Different machine learning models are tested with subject-wise grouped 10-fold cross-validation to avoid any data leakage from the subject level: EEG epochs and T-F images of the same subject are not included in the training and test sets. With the use of this strict assessment system, K-nearest neighbor classifier achieves an impressive accuracy, precision, sensitivity and specificity of 99.7%. Obtained findings show the promise of T-F representations based on SPWVD and deep features for subject-independent MDD detection.

Introduction
Depression can be considered as a prevalent mental disorder. According to a survey, it is estimated that 5% of people globally suffer from this condition. It can be identified by ongoing sadness and a decline in interest or enjoyment in once rewarding or enjoyable activities [1]. It may also affect appetite and sleep. Some frequent symptoms include Tiredness and lack of attention. The leading cause of disability in the world is depression, which adds greatly to the burden of disease [2]. When depression persists for two or more weeks or longer, it is referred to be Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) [3]. A person’s ability to function and lead a satisfying life can be significantly impacted by the long-lasting or recurrent effects of depression. There are a lot of effective treatments available to diagnose depression, but this type of treatment costs more. Due to this, people with low or middle income are not able to afford this type of treatment, also people with depression are not diagnosed properly and others who are not suffering from this are often misguided. The presence of at least five symptoms, including difficulty in sleeping, lack of interest, inability to focus, feelings of worthlessness, and depression, is required for the clinical diagnosis of MDD. Many contributing factors may lead to depression. In several reports, it has been seen that after the coronavirus pandemic, there has been a sudden increase in patients with depression [4], and the main events that may be responsible for this are unemployment, bereavement, and some traumatic events. This might make the affected person’s life circumstances worse by causing stress and dysfunction. Electroencephalogram (EEG) represents the electrical activity of the brain signals [5]. EEG can be used to detect and diagnose diseases related to the brain like sleep disorders, depressive disorders, and any kind of blood clotting in the brain. EEG signals are used to detect MDD, although there are several other methods available to detect MDD like ECG signals and speech signals. However, in many research works, it has been found that more accurate and consistent results are obtained through EEG signals [5]. In addition, it has been reported a visible change can be found in the delta rhythm of the MDD patient as compared to normal patients [6]. EEG signals contain pathological and physiological information, and changes can be seen in EEG signals for different mental states. In [7] authors said that the brain areas of people with depression had altered, reducing the patients’ decision-making abilities. According to [8], they were successful in differentiating between bipolar depression patients and healthy controls using an analysis of variance model. In the resting state, theta activity is particularly distinct at the central electrode position. By utilizing the EEG signals’ frequency property, the identification and medication of brain disorders can be done by taking the EEG signals. In [9] authors, utilized a genetic algorithm that picks multidimensional aspects of the EEG data and then fed only the chosen features into a classifier with a back propagation neural network to discriminate individuals with depression from normal persons. This approach has an accuracy of 89.12%. In [10] authors, investigated the efficacy of extracting non-linear characteristics from the EEGs of depressed people and healthy people. This study uses a feature selection method on the retrieved and non-uniform data before feeding them into a classifier for depressed and healthy person categorization. Liao et al [11] extracted features from EEG data using the nuclear feature filter bank’s public space pattern and principal component analysis they reported an 81.23% diagnosis accuracy. In [12], authors estimated the entropy of 16-channel EEG signals as the function and constructed a fuzzy logic method as a classifier, which achieved an accuracy of 87.5% in the categorization test of people suffering from severe depression and healthy persons. Deep learning approaches have largely supplanted standard machine learning methods in biological signal processing in recent years, this method, as opposed to ordinary machine learning, involves hidden structures consisting of multiple layers that may extract effective characteristics automatically. Furthermore, this method can be used to better approximate complicated functions and analyze data with high dimensionality and non-linearity, avoiding the issue of limited diagnostic capacities. Acharya et al. [13] employed a deep learning algorithm with a 13-layered CNN model to diagnose depression in 2018 and got relatively higher accuracy. In [14] authors introduced a two-layered long short-term memory (LSTM) model to retrieve quantitative parameters such as standard deviation, mean, skewness, and kurtosis, from input EEG signals received from the database. The characteristics were then used in the proposed model, which shows significant improvement as compared to prior techniques. Choudhary et al. [15] employed maximum likelihood estimation for MDD patients detection. Several studies have been performed using EEG signals and CNN models to achieve higher accuracy in the classification of MDD patients and Healthy patients. In [16] authors used end-to-end features and implemented a CNN-based model to achieve higher accuracy. In [17] authors used power features of EEG signals and has used a conformal kernel SVM for classification.
In this paper, a channel redundant and feature selection-based method is implemented and then a machine learning classifier is used for further processing to overcome the above-discussed challenges. The proposed model is found to be performing superior (as evident in the result section) as compared to the many methods of literature. In recent years, considerable advances have been made in the detection of MDD utilizing machine learning and EEG sensor data. However, many recent research uses classic machine learning models, which necessitate substantial feature engineering and operator intervention. Our suggested system distinguishes itself by including a sophisticated CNN specifically intended to automatically extract and evaluate complex characteristics from T-F images of raw EEG data, reducing the requirement for manual preprocessing and feature selection. Furthermore, we use a hybrid technique that combines CNN and machine learning modules to capture both spatial and temporal patterns in EEG data, which improves the model’s accuracy and robustness. To the best of our knowledge, no one has previously investigated this integrated technique for MDD identification using EEG data, which combines CNN and machine learning modules. This new combination not only speeds up the detection process but also increases diagnosis accuracy, making it an important contribution to the field of automated mental health diagnoses.

Data Set
EEG signals were recorded from healthy controls and patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) were recorded from a publicly available data set [18]. The data were obtained from the EEG of 34 depressed and 30 healthy subjects aged 12 to 77 years at the Hospital Universiti Sains Malaysia (HUSM). The EEG signals were recorded at 256 Hz. Three types of recordings were included: eyes closed EEG, eyes open EEG and task based EEG. Participants were instructed to close their eyes or keep them open for 5 minutes, reducing eye movements and eye blinking and head movements for the eyes-closed and eyes-open recordings [18]. In the task-based recordings, participants were shown a 10-minute visual stimulus, and asked to press the “SPACE” key when they saw a light on the screen. In this study, the EEG recording was divided into a number of segments, each segment being assigned the same identification number and diagnostic label as the corresponding participant. Signal preprocessing, generation of SPWVD images, feature extraction and classification preserved subject identifiers. This procedure enabled subject-wise partitioning of data and avoided the distribution of EEG segments or time-frequency images of the same subject in train and test sets.

T-F spectral images
Raw EEG is complex and nonstationary, so the analysis of raw data is time consuming. Hence, processing the input EEG signals in the time-frequency (T-F) domain is essential to obtain more meaningful and deep features of the signal. The following T-F representation shows how the amplitude and frequency of a time varying signal change with time [19]. One-dimensional EEG segments were used to create T-F images using the smoothed pseudo Wigner-Ville distribution (SPWVD) approach.
SPWVD
The smoothed pseudo Wigner-Ville distribution (SPWVD) is an effective time-frequency transformation technique that provides detailed information about the energy localization of a signal in both the time and frequency domains. It enables the analysis of higher harmonics and supports accurate time-domain localization. Feature Extraction for MDD Diagnosis Using pretrained models
The pretrained deep learning model extracts several critical features necessary for accurate diagnosis and classification of MDD before passing the processed data to the classifier. These extracted features can be broadly categorized into the following types:
Edge and Contour Properties: In its early layers, the pretrained deep learning model captures fundamental features such as edges, contours, and textures. These properties play a crucial role in distinguishing between the T-F images of normal and MDD classes.
Texture and Color Patterns: As data propagates through deeper layers, the model learns to identify increasingly complex textures and color patterns. Since T-F spectrograms manifest as variations in texture and color, these patterns are essential for differentiating between healthy and MDD classes.
Shape and Structural Features: The pretrained model further extracts shape-related and structural characteristics, which help in recognizing the overall morphology of spectrograms as well as specific changes induced by MDD.
High-Level Representations: In the final layers before classification, the model abstracts the data into high-level representations. These representations integrate edge, texture, color, and structural information, forming a comprehensive feature set that effectively characterizes the spectrograms.
The extracted features are then fed into the classifier, which utilizes this rich feature set to make precise predictions regarding the presence of MDD. By leveraging the extensive and hierarchical feature representations learned by the model, the classifier can accurately distinguish between normal and MDD classes.
Subject-Wise Grouped Cross-Validation
The generalization efficacy of the proposed framework is evaluated by a 10-fold cross-validation method incorporating subject categorization. The sample comprised 64 individuals, categorized into 10 distinct folds, each including about equal numbers of MDD sufferers and healthy controls. All EEG epochs and SPWVD time-frequency representations for each subject were assigned to the identical fold. In each iteration of the cross-validation, nine folds were utilized to construct the model, while a single fold was reserved exclusively for testing. The EEG epoch, time-frequency representation, or feature vector of any test subject was excluded from the training dataset. Every iteration of the investigation, totaling 10, included a single test subject. All data-dependent tasks, including as feature normalization, feature selection, classifier training, and hyperparameter tuning, were conducted solely on the training subset of each fold. The parameters derived from the training participants were subsequently utilized on the test subjects that were not exposed during the training phase. This procedure inhibited the dissemination of information from test subjects into the model’s development. To ensure an equitable comparison among the examined models and to assess all CNN architectures, a consistent subject-wise fold assignment was applied across all models. Each test fold was examined independently, and the outcomes are reported as the average and standard deviation of the 10 folds.
Transfer Learning
In this study, we employ transfer learning rather than training of a pretrained model from scratch. Training a benchmark model from the scratch requires a large-scale labeled dataset and substantial computational resources. Given the limited dataset size and hardware constraints, training a model from scratch is challenging and often impractical. To overcome these limitations and enhance model performance, we leverage transfer learning, which enables the adaptation of a pre-trained model to a new task. Transfer learning is a representation-based learning technique that repurposes knowledge from previously trained models for new applications. Typically, these models are trained on large datasets such as ImageNet, which contains 1.2 million images across 1,000 classes [21]. In our study, we utilize four benchmark pre-trained models: ResNet18, ResNet50, VGG, and AlexNet. The last three layers of these models are frozen, and the learned weight matrices are extracted as features for traditional machine learning classifiers. This hybrid approach allows us to leverage the rich feature representations learned by these deep networks while optimizing computational efficiency. Classification
According to the literature, the majority of studies involving EEG signals use different classifiers, such as SVM, KNN, and DT, as classification techniques. The effectiveness of these three classifiers—SVM, KNN, and DT—was examined in the suggested study. All the classifiers are validated by a 10-fold cross validation technique. Support Vector Machine - SVM creates decision making constraints that are linear in the modified region, supported by the kernel function, and a portion of the data specifically provides. SVM develop a classifier of maximum limit that widens the distance between the support vectors and the border of judgement. SVMs reduce the overfitting phenomena since they are guided by form and by design, which increases the margin of classifier. K-Nearest Neighbors - One of the straightforward identification model is the supervised classifier K-Nearest Neighbors. KNN makes use of an in-space function for pairings observation. For a test sample, the KNN method finds the training sample which is nearest to the distance k. The testing sample is then placed in a class that is frequently the most common class across the whole set of k-nearest training data. The KNN algorithm simply needs two inputs: a distance measure and a numeric value for the variable k. Decision tree - The decision tree is the most efficient and popular classification and prediction method. Flowchart-like tree structures called decision trees have core nodes that represent tests on attributes, branches that reflect test results, and leaf nodes (terminal nodes) that represent class labels, this comes under the supervised learning category and are non-parametric, repeatedly divide the space of features into regions that correspond to the classes by choosing a feature, This will afterwards deliver the elevated knowledge gain. The minimal number of specimens per unit of a decision tree is stretched to the value of two, at which point the recursive partitioning of the feature space ends.
Results
In this study, first the results are obtained directly using different CNN models and then a feature extraction-based method is used in which features are extracted from CNN models and then those features are fed into a Machine learning classifier for classification and in both methods, 10-fold cross-validation approach is used for model evaluation. The T-F resolution is mostly dependent on the window length; longer windows increase frequency resolution at the expense of time resolution, while shorter windows provide greater time resolution but poor frequency resolution. A window of moderate length achieves equilibrium by reducing overlap and increasing both time and frequency resolution. We used a Kaiser window of size 255 to acquire the SPWVD time-frequency spectrum pictures for our investigation. This decision was taken in order to ensure precise feature extraction from the EEG signals by optimizing the trade-off between temporal and frequency resolution. The models investigated were re-evaluated with subject wise grouped 10-fold cross validation. The test set consisted of only those who had not been included in the corresponding training set in each fold. This resulted in the fact that epochs and SPWVD images from the same participant could not be in both sets. All the models/classifiers were split by the same subject partitions. All performance reported is the average and standard deviation of the 10 test folds. The evaluation is done based on this subject-independent evaluation, which is more stringent than random image-level cross-validation, where the model had to classify the EEG data of new persons (not the same persons who had participated in the training session) but rather the new epochs of the same persons. In order to evaluate predictions, we utilized both image prediction and subject prediction. To determine performance at the image level, we used the forecasts for each SPWVD picture taken by the subjects that were kept out. To create a single diagnostic judgment for each participant in a subject level evaluation, the image-level predictions were integrated using mean predicted probability. Considering the framework’s intended application in diagnosing an unknown individual, the emphasis was on the subjects’ performance for this reason.

Performance of Various Machine Learning Kernels on Features Extracted Through the ResNet-18 Model
It is observed from the table that the Cubic SVM, Weighted KNN and Neural Network Classifier gave highest classification accuracy of 99.7%. Of these classifiers, the Weighted KNN classifier displayed very balanced performance with 99.7% accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score. This suggests that the Weighted KNN was equally good in discriminating MDD patients and HC subjects.
The Cubic SVM also showed good performance with an accuracy of 99.7%, a precision of 99.6%, a sensitivity of 99.9%, a specificity of 99.6%, and an F1 score of 99.7%. The high sensitivity value shows that Cubic SVM was very effective with regard to the correct identification of cases in MDD. Likewise, the Neural Network Classifier performed well, yielding an accuracy of 99.7% and an F1-score of 99.7%, indicating its effectiveness in the classification task.
The other Ensemble Bagged Tree classifier also had good performance with the accuracy of 99.2% and F1 score of 99.3%. The results were competitive for Quadratic SVM and Medium Gaussian SVM, with accuracies of 98.8% and 98.3% respectively. Linear SVM and Fine Gaussian SVM were relatively inferior in performance than other classifiers, however. Fine Gaussian SVM showed 100% sensitivity, but 90.7% specificity, which means that it was able to classify all the cases of MDD as MDD, but a larger number of healthy subjects as MDD.
Overall, the results show that the deep features extracted from ResNet-18 are highly discriminative for MDD detection. Among these classifiers, Cubic SVM, Weighted KNN and Neural Network Classifier achieved the best performance, with Weighted KNN classifier giving the most balanced and consistent classification results in all the metrics.
Discussion
Several studies have been done on automated depression detection with EEG signals using deep learning networks, machine learning techniques, LSTM layers-based CNN models, but most of the studies have been done using CNN models. This shows that CNN-based models are taking an edge over other techniques and can detect EEG features to accurately classify MDD patients and healthy patients. Most of the studies have employed feature extraction methods, in which they have broken down the EEG signal into sub-frequency bands such as delta (0.5-4 Hz), theta (4-8 Hz), alpha (8-13 Hz), and beta (13-30 Hz).
Table 3 provides a comparison between the proposed method and existing EEG-based MDD detection methods found in literature. The comparisons are made with respect to feature extraction technique, classification method, size of datasets and classification accuracy obtained. Various handcrafted feature extraction techniques, including wavelet transform (WT), power features, spectral features, structural connectivity features and predictive linear coding, have been applied to several previous studies. For instance, using an MLPNN classifier with WT features, Mahato et al. had an accuracy of  while using an SVM classifier with WT features, Mumtaz et al. had an accuracy of . In the same manner, Wu et al. employed a power features and conformal kernel SVM and achieved the accuracy of 83.64%. There has also been a great deal of research focused on the application of deep learning techniques to automatic MDD detection. CNN-based features were extracted by Acharya et al. and their accuracy was reported as 95.49% and 93.54% for right and left hemispheres respectively. By using a dataset of 34 patients with MDD and 30 healthy controls, Mumtaz et al. used 1D-CNN model and obtained a relatively high accuracy of 98.32%. Similarly, other CNN related studies reported accuracy of 85.62%, 92.66%, and 94.13% by Li et al., Uyulan et al., and Duan et al., respectively.
The proposed work has the highest classification accuracy of 99.7% compared to the existing ones. In the proposed framework, the EEG signals were converted into time-frequency (T-F) images, and CNN based deep features were extracted and finally classified by the KNN classifier. The enhanced performance suggests that the utilization of T-F image representation, deep feature extraction, and machine learning for classification is a very promising method for discriminating between patients with MDD and healthy controls.

Even though our presented approach showed better results for automated MDD detection using EEG data. The limitations of this work are:
The size and diversity of the dataset used to train and verify our model may have limited its ability to represent the range of EEG patterns found in larger populations. Our findings may be more broadly applicable in future research with a bigger and more varied dataset.
Although the proposed model achieved improved accuracy, interpretability remained an issue, as machine learning technologies are often considered as "black boxes." Further research into interpretable model designs or explain ability methodologies may increase clinician trust and acceptance.
Collaboration with healthcare professionals will be necessary to integrate the system seamlessly into existing diagnostic workflows and ensure its utility in clinical practice.
Conclusion
In this study, deep learning models are initially employed to detect MDD patients, but the achieved accuracy is comparatively low. Subsequently, a feature extraction-based technique is utilized, where features from a CNN model are extracted, and a machine learning classifier is used to further distinguish between MDD patients and healthy control participants. The suggested approach offers several benefits. Firstly, it exhibits greater accuracy in distinguishing between depression patients and healthy participants compared to existing approaches using the same dataset. Secondly, the method relies on feature extraction techniques that consume fewer computational resources, resulting in shorter training times and improved model efficiency. The achieved results through this method demonstrate the model’s high performance. Numerous studies have been conducted on the automated detection of major depressive disorder using deep learning approaches and machine learning methods. This indicates that CNN models are highly capable of extracting EEG features to accurately classify between MDD patients and healthy controls. To make this technique suitable for real-time clinical usage, we intend to reduce SPWVD’s computational demands and improve the model’s processing speed.
